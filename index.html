Why generic advice fails

The same telescope means two different hobbies.

Most buying guides rank telescopes by aperture and price alone, as if every backyard sees the same sky. It doesn't. An 8-inch Dobsonian built to pull in faint galaxies is wasted under a city glow that swallows anything dimmer than the Moon. A small refractor in genuinely dark countryside, meanwhile, is leaving real detail on the table. The sky above your specific address is the first spec that matters; before aperture, the brand, and before the budget.

The Bortle scale

Nine classes, one very different night each time.

Developed by John Bortle in 2001, the scale runs from Class 1 (the darkest skies left on Earth) to Class 9 (inner-city glow). A few reference points:

Class 1–2

SQM 21.7–22.0 · ~3,000+ stars · Milky Way casts shadows

Best targets: everything, including the faintest galaxies and nebulae.

→

Class 3–4

SQM 20.5–21.6 · ~2,000 stars · Milky Way still detailed

Best targets: deep-sky objects, star clusters, the Milky Way's structure.

→

Class 5–6

SQM 19.1–20.4 · ~500 stars · Milky Way washed out

Best targets: brighter clusters, double stars, planets. Filters start to help.

→

Class 7

SQM 18.5–19.1 · ~200–300 stars · No Milky Way

Best targets: Moon, planets, the brightest deep-sky objects only.

→

Class 8–9

SQM <18.5 · ~100–200 stars · Constant sky glow

Best targets: Moon and planets. Deep-sky work needs a dark-sky trip.

Sample recommendations

Compact, holds collimation on a balcony, and does justice to the Moon and planets.

Suburb Bortle 6

Enough aperture for brighter clusters and double stars, still portable.

Small town Bortle 4

8" Dobsonian

Dark-sky park Bortle 2

10–12" Dobsonian

Buying guidance

What actually matters, in order.

Aperture gathers light — bigger means fainter, more detailed objects, right up until your sky's brightness caps what more aperture can reveal. Past that point, extra inches just add weight and cost. That's why portability and aperture pull against each other, and the correct trade-off depends entirely on your sky and how often you'll actually carry the thing outside.

Dobsonian reflector

Most aperture per dollar. Best where dark sky is available and portability isn't the constraint.

Refractor

Sharp, low-maintenance, grab-and-go. Best for Moon, planets, and city or suburban skies.

SCT / Maksutov

Compact for its aperture, works well with a GoTo mount. Good middle ground for mixed targets.

Binoculars

Wide, forgiving views and zero setup. Often the right first instrument, in any sky.

Filters help most in Bortle 5 and brighter, narrowing the wavelengths a nebula emits against a bright background. GoTo mounts matter more as your object list grows past what you can star-hop to by hand — useful anywhere, essential nowhere.

Limitations & honest notes

The map is a starting point, not ground truth.

Satellite-derived light pollution data describes your region, not your exact backyard. A treeline, a neighbor's floodlight, or a house blocking your western horizon can matter more locally than anything a map shows. Where possible, confirm with a naked-eye star count or a handheld sky quality meter at your actual observing spot before buying around a number alone.

About this project

Built to prevent one specific kind of regret.

This guide exists because too many first telescopes get returned or shelved. Not because they're bad instruments, but because they were matched to the wrong sky. It pairs satellite light-pollution data with the Bortle dark-sky scale to give a recommendation grounded in your actual conditions.

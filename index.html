Why generic advice fails

The same telescope means two different hobbies.

Most buying guides rank telescopes by aperture and price alone, as if every backyard sees the same sky. It doesn't. An 8-inch Dobsonian built to pull in faint galaxies is wasted under a city glow that swallows anything dimmer than the Moon. A small refractor in genuinely dark countryside, meanwhile, is leaving real detail on the table. The sky above your specific address is the first spec that matters: before aperture, before the brand, before the budget.

The Bortle scale

Nine classes, one very different night each time.

Developed by John Bortle in 2001, the scale runs from Class 1 (the darkest skies left on Earth) to Class 9 (inner-city glow). A few reference points:

Class 1–2

SQM 21.7–22.0 · ~3,000+ stars · Milky Way casts shadows

Best targets: everything, including the faintest galaxies and nebulae.

Start with 10–12″ Dobsonian

→

Class 3–4

SQM 20.5–21.6 · ~2,000 stars · Milky Way still detailed

Best targets: deep-sky objects, star clusters, the Milky Way's structure.

Start with 8″ Dobsonian

→

Class 5–6

SQM 19.1–20.4 · ~500 stars · Milky Way washed out

Best targets: brighter clusters, double stars, planets. Filters start to help.

Start with 6″ Dobsonian or 5″ SCT

→

Class 7

SQM 18.5–19.1 · ~200–300 stars · No Milky Way

Best targets: Moon, planets, the brightest deep-sky objects only.

Start with 5–6″ refractor or small SCT

→

Class 8–9

SQM <18.5 · ~100–200 stars · Constant sky glow

Best targets: Moon and planets. Deep-sky work needs a dark-sky trip.

Start with 4–5″ refractor or Maksutov

Know the instruments

Telescope types, explained for beginners.

The four designs you’ll meet in most buying guides: what they are, how they behave, and which sky each one suits.

Reflector · alt-az mount

Dobsonian

A Newtonian reflector tube sitting on a simple swivel base (invented by John Dobson). You push it by hand to track the sky. No motors, no alignment ritual; just point and look.

They deliver the most aperture per rupee of any common design. The trade-off is bulk: an 8″ or 10″ Dob is a two-trip load for the car, and the open tube needs occasional collimation (aligning the mirrors).

Best for · dark or semi-dark skies · visual deep-sky · maximum light-gathering on a budget

Lens · sealed tube

Refractor

Light passes through a lens at the front. The tube is sealed, so there’s no collimation and almost no maintenance. Views of the Moon, planets, and double stars are typically sharp and high-contrast.

Achromats are affordable; apochromats (ED / apo) control colour better and cost more. Long tubes can be awkward on a mount, but small refractors are the classic grab-and-go scope for a balcony or a short walk outside.

Best for · city and suburban skies · Moon & planets · beginners who want zero fuss

Catadioptric · compact

SCT & Maksutov

Schmidt-Cassegrains (SCT) and Maksutov-Cassegrains fold the light path with mirrors and a corrector plate. You get a long focal length in a short, closed tube; easy to store and to put on a GoTo mount.

They’re slower to cool down than an open Dob, and the secondary obstruction softens contrast slightly versus a refractor. In return you get portability for the aperture and a natural fit with computerised mounts for finding faint targets.

Best for · mixed targets · GoTo users · travellers who need aperture in a small case

Two eyes · no setup

Binoculars

Not a telescope, but often the right first instrument. Handheld 7×50 or 10×50 binoculars show star fields, the brighter clusters, and the Moon’s texture with zero setup time. A cheap tripod adapter helps for longer looks.

They teach the sky without the learning curve of a mount. Many experienced observers still keep a pair permanently in the bag: wide, comfortable views that a narrow telescope field can’t match.

Best for · absolute beginners · any sky · travel and quick sessions

Aperture vs. portability

More aperture shows fainter objects only until your sky’s brightness caps what it can reveal. Past that, extra inches just add weight and cost.

Filters

Help most at Bortle 5 and brighter, by cutting the wavelengths of sky glow around a nebula.

GoTo mounts

Worth it once your target list outgrows star-hopping. Useful in any sky, essential in none.

Honest limits

Satellite data describes your region, not your backyard. A treeline, a neighbour’s floodlight or a building on your western horizon can matter more than anything a map shows.

Before buying around a number, confirm with a naked-eye star count or a sky quality meter where you’ll actually observe.
